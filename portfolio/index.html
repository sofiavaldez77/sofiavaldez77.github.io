PhD Research
Human-AI Interaction Generative AI Mixed Methods Interaction Design

More Interactivity in Human-AI Design Tools Doesn't Mean Better Outcomes. Here's Why.

TLDR: I built an interactive AI-powered structural design tool from scratch, generating my own dataset, training a generative AI model, and developing a suite of real-time interaction tools. The goal was to make structural design interactive, so designers could steer the AI toward holistic solutions rather than just selecting from what it generated. I then ran a controlled mixed-methods study with 79 participants, combining quantitative behavioral metrics with thematic and sentiment analysis of participant feedback. The counterintuitive finding: higher interactivity doesn't automatically mean higher perceived control or improved design outcomes like satisfaction and accuracy. What matters most is how the level of authorship users actually experience compares to what they expected going in.

79 participants in a controlled study where higher interactivity didn't predict better outcomes. Sequence and expectations did.

Case 01
Think-Aloud Usability Sessions Competitive Benchmarking Conversion Optimization Thematic Analysis

From "Definitely a Scam" to 2.5x Onboarding & Checkout Conversion

TLDR: Panda Mobile brought me in to redesign their onboarding funnel with a goal of doubling conversion. Through think-aloud usability sessions where customers walked through the product out loud, I found two things driving drop-off: an intent mismatch between what the ads promised and what the funnel delivered, and an overall ad-to-onboarding experience that incited hesitation and distrust at every stage. Rather than redesigning the funnel in isolation, I mapped the entire acquisition journey from ad to checkout and went beyond scope to collaborate with the marketing team alongside product, addressing trust and intent at every touchpoint. The onboarding redesign alone drove a 2.5x conversion lift in under a month.

Case 02
Generative Interviews Thematic Analysis Brand Strategy Stakeholder Navigation

What Started as Landing Page Testing Ended Up Shaping a Brand

TLDR: Four interviews in, I knew the research had potential well beyond the original brief. Panda Mobile had just defined their target audience for the first time, and I had recruited 50+ people from that audience to test landing page variations. But what I was hearing from participants had implications across product, marketing, and brand strategy that were too valuable to leave on the table. After navigating early stakeholder resistance, I expanded the scope and changed the interview questions to explore carrier switching motivations, pricing sensitivities, and feature preferences. I built strategy around what the team already had rather than adding net-new resources, turning constraints into advantages. One example: the team assumed their audience preferred family plans and were planning to build them out, a significant engineering investment. The interviews said otherwise. That insight became the "Bye Bye Family Plan!" campaign, turning the lack of family plans into a competitive edge. Most of those recommendations were implemented, and the brand still reflects them.

50+ interviews recruited in under 48 hours, with recommendations implemented across brand, product, and marketing that the team still references today

My Research & Design Process

People ask what mechanical engineering has to do with UX. The answer is everything. In hardware, once a product is manufactured and in someone's hands, there's no update you can push to fix a design mistake. That puts a serious premium on understanding user needs before a decision gets made, and doing it rigorously. The process I use is adapted from that training, and it applies well beyond hardware to digital products, brand strategy, or any problem where the goal is a better outcome for a real person.

01
Background Research
Getting oriented in the domain, the market, and the team's actual constraints before talking to any users.
Stakeholder Interviews Heuristic Review Analytics Patent Search Literature Review
02
Customer Interviews & Analysis
Open-ended but structured conversations and observation sessions to surface real needs and mental models.
Thematic Analysis Affinity Mapping Weighted Needs List Surveys Usability Studies
03
Competitive Benchmarking
Understanding how well competitors are actually meeting the needs I found in step two, and which design decisions get them there.
Product Walkthroughs SWOT Analysis Feature Benchmarking Needs-Based Scoring
04
Design Requirements
Translating customer needs into actionable, prioritized specs with stakeholder input and team constraints built in from the start.
House of Quality Requirements Matrix Stakeholder Alignment
05
Brainstorming
Divergent ideation through structured methods, then converging to a defensible direction.
Mind Mapping 6-3-5 Brainwriting Morph Matrix Pugh Chart
06
Prototyping
Making ideas tangible enough to test at whatever fidelity the question demands.
Wireframing Figma Interaction Design
07
Iteration
Refining through evidence, from interviews to usability tests to A/B tests, until the design earns its results.
Usability Testing A/B Testing Product Analytics Design Revisions

About Me

The Longer Version

I've always had a passion for creating and design, which is actually what drew me to mechanical engineering in the first place. What I didn't expect was that the part of the design process I'd come to love most wasn't the building — it was the conversations before anything got built. Every project I worked on came back to the same question: what do people actually need here? And it turns out mechanical engineering was about the most rigorous training I could have had for that instinct. In hardware, once a product is manufactured and in someone's hands, you can't push an update to fix a design mistake. That puts a serious premium on deeply understanding user needs before committing to a direction, and doing it systematically.

By the end of my undergrad, I had developed a specific design interest I couldn't shake: as AI started entering the design process, I wanted to understand what that would mean for the human's role in it. So before ChatGPT or Claude existed, that curiosity pushed me toward a PhD in human-AI interactive design, where I ended up building and evaluating an AI-powered generative design framework from the ground up. What I found was that design outcomes in human-AI collaboration aren't just about how interactive we build the human-AI tools to be. They're shaped by what people expect their role in that collaboration to be, and how that expectation is met.

Along the way I picked up a Business Foundations Minor, which keeps me anchored to outcomes that actually matter to a company. I'm drawn to problems that sit between disciplines, translating user needs into product strategy or helping a team see why a design decision will move a metric. That approach has worked in product, in brand strategy, and in engineering. I don't think it's domain-specific.

Right now I'm looking for a role where research has a real seat at the table and design decisions are made with intention. If that sounds like your team, I'd love to talk.
